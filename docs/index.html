03 / Concept 1
Framework Step 1 — Define It

Emerging Technology

Definition

New or developing technologies with the potential to disrupt industries or create entirely new markets.

Not all new technology is truly "emerging." The term refers specifically to technologies in early development that carry transformative potential — but whose full impact is still uncertain.

Evaluating emerging technology requires separating signal from noise — distinguishing technologies with genuine staying power from those that will fade.

04 / Concept 2
Framework Step 5 — Build Fluency

Digital Fluency

Definition

The ability to evaluate, adopt, and adapt to digital technologies with confidence — going beyond basic literacy to active, strategic engagement.

Digital literacy means you can use technology. Digital fluency means you can evaluate it, question it, and make strategic decisions about it — even when it's unfamiliar.

Literacy (baseline)

Can use existing tools. Follows instructions. Dependent on others for evaluation.

↓   Grows into
Fluency (goal)

Evaluates new tools independently. Asks the right questions. Adapts to change with confidence and leads others through it.

"The illiterate of the 21st century will not be those who cannot read and write, but those who cannot learn, unlearn, and relearn." — Alvin Toffler

05 / Concept 3
Framework Step 4 — Assess the Timeline

Technology Roadmap

Definition

A strategic planning tool that outlines how technology capabilities will evolve over time — aligning technology decisions with business objectives.

A roadmap answers the question: "When do we need to act?" It helps leaders sequence technology investments and avoid both premature adoption and costly delay.

Roadmaps are used by organizations to plan infrastructure upgrades, software adoption, and capability development across months and years.

Framework Step 3 — Map the Impact

Disruptive Innovation

Definition

Technologies or business models that fundamentally change how industries operate — displacing established incumbents and creating new market leaders.

Coined by Clayton Christensen, disruption typically starts at the low end or edges of a market before moving upmarket. Incumbents often ignore it — until it is too late.

"Disruption is not about technology. It's about the business model change technology enables."

Who Got Disrupted — and By What

A model describing how innovations spread across five adopter categories — from early risk-takers to the last wave of mainstream users.

The "Chasm" (Geoffrey Moore): the gap between Early Adopters and the Early Majority is where most technologies fail to scale.

08 / Concept 6
Framework Step 2 — Locate It

The Hype Cycle

Definition

A framework by Gartner describing the five stages of emerging technology adoption — from inflated expectations through disillusionment to eventual productive use.

The Hype Cycle helps leaders answer the question: "Is this technology where the headlines say it is?"

Innovation Trigger — A breakthrough sparks media coverage and early interest

Peak of Inflated Expectations — Hype exceeds reality; overinvestment follows

Trough of Disillusionment — Failures emerge; interest drops sharply

Slope of Enlightenment — Real-world benefits become clearer

Plateau of Productivity — Mainstream adoption, sustainable value

09 / Concept 7
Framework — Define and Locate

Blockchain

Definition

A distributed ledger enabling secure, transparent, and tamper-resistant transactions — without requiring a central authority to verify or record them.

Every transaction is grouped into a "block," cryptographically linked to the previous block, and replicated across thousands of nodes. Changing any record would require altering every subsequent block on every node simultaneously — making fraud effectively impossible.

Hype Cycle status: Blockchain is navigating the Trough of Disillusionment — early cryptocurrency hype has faded, but real enterprise applications in supply chain, finance, and identity are gaining traction.

Business Applications
Supply Chain Transparency

Walmart and IBM use blockchain to trace food origin from farm to shelf — reducing contamination response time from 7 days to 2 seconds.

Smart Contracts

Self-executing agreements that trigger automatically when conditions are met — removing intermediaries from real estate, insurance, and legal transactions.

Digital Identity

Secure, portable identity verification without dependence on government or corporate databases.

10 / Concept 8
Framework — Define and Assess Timeline

Quantum Computing

Definition

A computing paradigm that uses principles of quantum mechanics — superposition and entanglement — to process information in fundamentally new ways, solving problems classical computers cannot.

Classical computers use bits (0 or 1). Quantum computers use qubits, which can exist in multiple states simultaneously, enabling them to explore millions of solutions at once.

Timeline reality check: Quantum computing is on the Innovation Trigger phase of the Hype Cycle. Practical, widespread business applications are likely 10–15 years away — but the implications for cryptography and security are immediate planning concerns.

11 / Concept 9
Framework — Define and Map the Impact

Extended Reality (XR)

Definition

An umbrella term for technologies that blend digital and physical environments — including Virtual Reality (VR), Augmented Reality (AR), and Mixed Reality (MR).

🥽
Virtual Reality (VR)

Fully Immersive Digital World

Completely replaces physical environment. Applications: training simulations, medical procedures, architecture walkthroughs, immersive entertainment.

📲
Augmented Reality (AR)

Digital Overlaid on Physical

Adds digital elements to the real world. Applications: retail try-ons, navigation overlays, surgical guidance, warehouse picking systems (Amazon, Boeing).

🌐
Mixed Reality (MR)

Digital Anchored to Physical

Digital objects interact with the physical world in real time. Applications: Microsoft HoloLens for engineering, remote expert assistance, spatial computing.

Business impact: XR is projected to reshape training, retail, healthcare, and remote collaboration — with the enterprise market expected to exceed $125 billion by 2030.

12 / Concept 10
Framework Step 5 — Build Fluency

Career Agility

Definition

The ability to pivot career paths and adapt to changing technological and market conditions — treating your career as a dynamic portfolio, not a fixed ladder.

The half-life of a job skill is now estimated at less than 5 years in technology-adjacent fields. Career agility is not about predicting the future — it is about building the capacity to adapt to it.

The concepts in this course are tools for career agility. Being able to evaluate blockchain, XR, or quantum computing does not mean you must work in those fields — it means you will not be surprised or displaced by them.

🔍

Scan Continuously

Follow emerging technology through trusted sources (Gartner, MIT Tech Review, CB Insights). Build a habit of curious observation, not passive consumption.

🧪

Experiment Early

Early Adopters shape how technologies are used. Use the Adoption Lifecycle — identify when to experiment (Early Majority phase) before your industry demands it.

🔗

Build Adjacent Skills

Combine domain expertise with digital fluency. A marketer who understands AI, or an accountant who understands blockchain, is exponentially more valuable.
